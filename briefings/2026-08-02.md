## Wildcard

### [UPDATE] EU AI Act enforcement is live — Article 50 takes effect today

The European Commission's AI Office and national market surveillance authorities began enforcing the EU AI Act on August 2, 2026 as scheduled.

**Background:** Our July 31 entry covered the scope in detail — Article 50 transparency obligations (chatbot disclosure, deepfake labeling, AI-generated audio/video marking) apply to providers, and the AI Office holds direct enforcement authority over GPAI model providers. High-risk AI compliance was deferred to December 2027 via the Omnibus agreement.

**New:** The Commission issued a formal press release confirming the enforcement start. The AI Office is now empowered to request technical documentation, impose corrective measures, and issue fines — up to €15M / 3% of global turnover for transparency violations, up to €35M / 7% for the most serious breaches. This is the first day the fines are legally actionable, not just threatened.

Sources: [European Commission press corner](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1714), [EU Digital Strategy](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-starts-enforcing-ai-act-rules-and-new-transparency-requirements-2-august), [Olakai explainer](https://olakai.ai/blog/eu-ai-act-enforcement-august-2026/).

### [NEW] California SB 942 operative today — genAI content detection required

California's AI Transparency Act (SB 942) became operative August 2, 2026, after AB 853 (October 2025) pushed the date from the original January 1, 2026.

**Who it hits:** "Covered Providers" — entities that create or produce a generative AI system with 1M+ monthly California users that is publicly accessible within the state. That captures essentially every major genAI product.

**Requirements in force today:**
- Free, publicly available genAI content detection tool that lets users assess whether image, video, or audio content was created or altered by the provider's system.
- Visible and invisible marking (C2PA-compatible) of synthetic media.
- Manifest statements and public disclosure to end-users and downstream licensees.

**Penalties:** California AG enforces; civil penalties for violations including failure to provide detection tools.

**Hosting platforms** (YouTube, X, etc.) have until January 1, 2027 to comply with their own obligations.

Sources: [Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/22782-implemented-california-ai-transparency-act-sb-942), [Jones Day explainer](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2024/10/california-enacts-ai-transparency-law-requiring-disclosures-for-ai-content), [TrustArc compliance guide](https://trustarc.com/resource/california-ai-transparency-laws-sb942-ab2013/).

### [NEW] Chinese threat actor used DeepSeek to autonomously attack 460+ servers — Claude and OpenAI both refused

Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 published a report (July 30, 2026) detailing the first in-the-wild case of an autonomous AI-driven cyberattack campaign against internet-facing production systems.

**Threat actor:** Aliases "knaithe" / "KnYuan," assessed to be based in Zhuhai, China and self-described as a "binary security researcher." Active on GitHub and security forums.

**Technical setup:** DeepSeek served as the reasoning engine behind [Hermes Agent](https://github.com/HKUDS/Hermes), an open-source AI framework with OS terminal access, command execution, and internet connectivity. "Yolo mode" allows autonomous execution of risky commands without human confirmation. The operator initiated the campaign via a single Telegram message.

**Attack pipeline (fully autonomous after initial command):**
1. Enumerate and scan targets
2. Research applicable CVEs
3. Pull public exploit code
4. Attempt exploitation

**Scale and confirmed impact:** 460+ targets scanned. Unit 42 confirmed data exfiltration from three Citrix NetScaler appliances (CVE-2026-3055) and command execution on 11 Marimo notebook endpoints (CVE-2026-39987).

**Discovery:** Hermes accidentally spun up a web server from its home directory, exposing the attacker's environment — API keys, exploit scripts, target lists, shell history, and AI attack logs.

**The AI safety angle:** The threat actor attempted the same attack requests against Claude and OpenAI before settling on DeepSeek. Both refused. DeepSeek did not. This is the first publicly documented case of an AI model being chosen specifically because it lacked safety guardrails for offensive security use.

**[RUMOR]** Unit 42 assessed the actor's autonomous attacks did not achieve confirmed compromise on the majority of targets; manual exploitation achieved the confirmed impact on the three Citrix targets. The distinction between "AI-assisted manual" and "fully autonomous" success remains unclear in the report.

Sources: [Unit 42 / Palo Alto Networks](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/autonomous-ai-cyber-attack-campaign/), [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hacker-uses-deepseek-ai-to-autonomously-attack-vulnerable-servers/), [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/chinese-hacker-commands-deepseek-via.html), [TechTimes (Claude/OpenAI refused angle)](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322582/20260801/deepseek-ran-autonomous-cyberattacks-that-claude-openai-safety-controls-blocked.htm).

**Why it matters:** This is not a theoretical attack — it happened at scale with confirmed exploitation. For anyone building agentic systems with shell/network access, this is the concrete threat model. Yolo mode + uncensored open-weight model = working offensive agent. The OPSEC failure (accidental web server) is the only reason this surfaced.
